# Demand for, and publication landscape of, numerical methods for stochastic differential games with asymmetric or private information

Research date: 18 November 2026. Verification status is marked per item. "Opened" means the page or arXiv abstract was fetched and read; "search summary" means the fact comes from a search-engine summary of a page that could not be opened directly (SIAM, Wiley and INFORMS pages returned HTTP 403), and should be re-checked before it is cited in a paper.

## Key question 1: Who would use such a method, and for what? Published calls for numerical methods, or statements that no tractable solution is known

### Takeaway
The clearest demand is in the broker–informed-trader literature of the Jaimungal, Cartea and Sánchez-Betancourt groups, where the partial-information Nash equilibrium is characterised by an FBSDE system "that involves the two differing filtrations" but is not solved, and in the control-theory literature on LQG games with partial and asymmetric information, which states in print that no algorithm exists for the coupled estimation–control equations and that "no known sequential decomposition method exists" once the common-information structure is dropped. Outside finance, the same need is stated for asymmetric-information stochastic games in cyber-physical systems and for partially observed mean-field control.

### Cited Findings

Finance: brokers, informed traders, private signals
- Wu and Jaimungal, "Broker-Trader Partial Information Nash-Equilibria", arXiv:2412.17712 (v1 23 Dec 2024, v2 1 Apr 2025, no journal reference listed). An informed trader with a trading signal trades multiple assets with a broker who "only observes aggregate prices and cannot distinguish between underlying trends and volatility". The equilibrium "corresponds to the solution of a nonstandard system of forward-backward stochastic differential equations (FBSDEs) that involves the two differing filtrations"; existence and uniqueness are shown by convex analysis, and for short horizons the system has a unique solution with polynomial approximation properties. The equilibrium is characterised, not solved explicitly or numerically. Opened. — [arXiv:2412.17712](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.17712)
- Aqsha, Drissi and Sánchez-Betancourt, "Strategic Learning and Trading in Broker-Mediated Markets", arXiv:2412.20847 (v1 30 Dec 2024, revised 19 Jan 2026; listed as working paper on Sánchez-Betancourt's page). Verbatim: "To the best of our knowledge, this paper, together with Wu and Jaimungal (2024), is among the first to study settings in which both liquidity providers and takers optimise their strategies while filtering missing information. While Wu and Jaimungal (2024) analyse the Nash equilibrium in a multidimensional setting, our approach adopts a Stackelberg-type formulation." Both agents use Kalman–Bucy filters; strategies are derived explicitly "owing to the linear–quadratic structure". Economic finding: "Information leakage in the client's trading flow generates economic value for the broker" comparable to transaction costs. Full text opened. — [arXiv:2412.20847](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.20847); [research page](https://leandro-sbetancourt.github.io/research.html)
- Cartea, Jaimungal and Sánchez-Betancourt, "Nash Equilibrium between Brokers and Traders", Finance and Stochastics 30(3), 951–982, 2026 (arXiv:2407.10561, v3 22 Jul 2025). Perfect-information game between a broker, an informed trader and an uninformed trader; the equilibrium is "characterized with the solution to a coupled system of forward-backward stochastic differential equations (FBSDEs)", solved explicitly. Venue and pages from the author's research page; arXiv page opened. — [arXiv:2407.10561](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10561); [research page](https://leandro-sbetancourt.github.io/research.html)
- Bergault and Sánchez-Betancourt, "A Mean Field Game between Informed Traders and a Broker", SIAM Journal on Financial Mathematics 16(2), 358–388, 2025 (arXiv:2401.05257). "Informed traders observe a common signal and a private signal"; the mean-field limit is solved in closed form via an FBSDE system "that we solve explicitly". The finite-player version is not solved in closed form (inference from the abstract, which gives closed forms only for the mean-field game). arXiv page opened; venue from the author's page. — [arXiv:2401.05257](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05257); [research page](https://leandro-sbetancourt.github.io/research.html)
- Cartea and Sánchez-Betancourt, "Brokers and Informed Traders: Dealing with Toxic Flow and Extracting Trading Signals", SIAM Journal on Financial Mathematics 16(2), 243–270, 2025. Venue from the author's page; not opened. — [research page](https://leandro-sbetancourt.github.io/research.html)
- Casgrain and Jaimungal, "Mean-field games with differing beliefs for algorithmic trading", Mathematical Finance 30, 995–1034, 2020 (arXiv:1810.06101). Nash equilibrium given by "a nonstandard vector-valued forward–backward stochastic differential equation"; the paper presents "a least square Monte Carlo based algorithm for computing the equilibria". Search summary of the Wiley page. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/mafi.12237)
- Hambly, Xu and Yang, "Linear-quadratic Gaussian Games with Asymmetric Information: Belief Corrections Using the Opponents Actions", arXiv:2307.15842 (29 Jul 2023, 34 pages). Two-player non-zero-sum LQG game where each player has private noisy measurements and observes opponent actions. Verbatim: "The challenge of this asymmetry is that it introduces correlations into the players' belief processes for the state and leads to circularity in their beliefs about their opponents beliefs." The paper restricts to linear policies and obtains a closed form, with a bargaining application. Opened. — [arXiv:2307.15842](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15842)

Kyle-type markets with several informed traders
- Choi and Larsen, "Existence and convergence of discrete-time Kyle models with multiple insiders", arXiv:2607.15057 (Jul 2026). Verbatim: "While both Kyle (1985) and Holden and Subrahmanyam (1992) give proofs ensuring that a Nash equilibrium exists, no existence proof is available to guarantee that the model in Foster and Viswanathan (1996) is supported by a Nash equilibrium." and "To reduce the model's complexity, Foster and Viswanathan (1996) place a symmetry assumption on the traders' information structure... The symmetry assumption produces a one dimensional second-order recursion. Because the dimension is one, we can use a shooting technique to prove existence of a solution." The discrete-time recursion converges to the ODE solution of Back, Cao and Willard (2000). Opened (HTML). — [arXiv:2607.15057](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.15057)
- A search summary of the same paper reported that one Kyle extension "has not been able to find a closed-form solution" and must be "solved numerically", but this sentence was not located on the opened page. Unverified.

Control theory: LQG games with partial and asymmetric information
- Guan, Shames and Summers, "Forward-Backward Dynamic Programming for LQG Dynamic Games with Partial and Asymmetric Information", arXiv:2603.18304 (Mar 2026; formatted as a journal research article, journal not identified). Verbatim: "The information asymmetry introduces fundamental challenges involving belief representation and theory of mind, where players must impute the belief states and estimates of other players to inform their strategy. This leads to an infinite regress of higher-order beliefs, which renders even representing strategies and value functions difficult." and "Dynamic games with partial and asymmetric information have also been studied since the 1960s ..., though far less extensively than dynamic games with perfect state information." On the coupled Riccati-type equations for estimation and control: "Once again, there are no existing algorithms to jointly solve these coupled equations to the best of our knowledge." They restrict to zero-sum LQG with limited internal state dimension and provide an open-source implementation. Full text opened. — [arXiv:2603.18304](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18304)
- Guan, Shames and Summers, "Best Response Dynamics for Zero-Sum Dynamic Games with Partial-Asymmetric Information", arXiv:2608.13755 (Aug 2026). Verbatim: "for general games lacking this common information structure, to the best of our knowledge, no known sequential decomposition method exists. Consequently, we do not provide a direct numerical comparison with common information-based sequential decomposition approaches in this paper, since these methods are not well defined for the private, noisy measurement setting studied here, and adapting them to this asymmetric structure remains an open problem in its own right." On a hidden-parameter extension: "no general closed-form solution to this class of problems exists even in the single-player LQG setting." Full text opened. — [arXiv:2608.13755](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13755)
- Li, Hammar, Stadler and Zhu, "Conjectural Online Learning with First-order Beliefs in Asymmetric Information Stochastic Games", arXiv:2402.18781 (v4 Aug 2024; cyber-physical systems, IT infrastructures). Verbatim: "Existing computational methods for AISGs are primarily offline and can not adapt to equilibrium deviations. Further, current methods are limited to particular information structures to avoid belief hierarchies." and "While the assumption that players share the same belief simplifies computations, it is not realistic for most practical scenarios." Full text opened. — [arXiv:2402.18781](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18781)
- Ralston, Yang and Hu, "Information Revelation and Alignment Faking in Stochastic Differential Games", arXiv:2603.17197 (v2 Sep 2026). Two-player LQ stochastic differential game under partial information with hidden cost parameters. The authors do not compute the partial-information equilibrium; they define "implementable controls by averaging the full-information Nash equilibrium under each player's prior" because "the full-information NE controls (3)–(4) depend on both parameters, they are generally not implementable in the partial-information setting." ### Inferences
- The two benchmark families of the proposed method (broker–trader, broker plus mean field of informed traders) come from papers published in Finance and Stochastics (2026) and SIFIN (2025). The authors of those papers are the natural first users, and Wu–Jaimungal's unsolved two-filtration FBSDE is the natural first target.
- The strongest printed statements of "no method exists" are in the control literature (Guan–Shames–Summers 2026, Li et al. 2024), not in finance. A finance-venue paper can cite them for motivation, but the control audience may ask for comparison with common-information or belief-truncation approaches.
- Every published partial-information game paper found solves its model either in closed form under LQ structure (Aqsha et al., Hambly–Xu–Yang, Bergault–SB) or stops at a characterisation (Wu–Jaimungal). None computes a non-LQ or non-closed-form equilibrium with non-nested filtrations. A method that does so fills a documented gap; a method validated only on LQ games does not yet demonstrate the gap is closed.
- Ralston–Yang–Hu (2026) is a current, direct example of researchers substituting a heuristic "implementable control" because the private-information equilibrium is not computable; such papers are the audience for a general solver.

### Gaps
- No finance paper found that states in print "a numerical method for games with non-nested filtrations is needed"; the demand is implicit (characterisation without solution) rather than explicit.
- The claim that multiple-insider Kyle models must be solved numerically could not be verified on the opened page.
- Evidence for energy and robotics applications with private information in continuous time is thin: one unopened preprint (arXiv:2606.19639) and the Hu–Laurière survey's list of application areas.
- No citation counts could be verified; search summaries gave Hambly–Xu–Yang about 493 citations and Casgrain–Jaimungal 85, both unverified.

## Key question 2: How were comparable methodological papers published and received?

### Takeaway
Deep-learning methods for stochastic differential games have been published across machine-learning conferences (MSML 2020, ICML 2021 and 2022), applied-probability and numerical-analysis journals (SIAM J. Numer. Anal. 2021, Ann. Appl. Probab. 2022), operations research (Math. Oper. Res. 2025), finance (Math. Finance 2020, 2023; SIFIN 2025; Finance and Stochastics 2026) and specialised journals (Frontiers of Mathematical Finance 2022; Numerical Algebra, Control and Optimization 2024). The pattern is: algorithm plus experiments at a conference or in SIFIN/Math. Finance, convergence theory in a separate paper. Several Jaimungal-group numerical-game papers remain arXiv preprints.

### Inferences
- Method papers with LQ or scalar examples and moderate theory have been accepted at ICML (Sig-DFP, 2021) and MSML (DFP, 2020) when the algorithmic idea was new; the convergence analysis followed in a mathematics journal (Frontiers of Mathematical Finance, 2022). The same two-paper split would suit "deep Picard regression": algorithm and finance examples in SIFIN or Math. Finance, fixed-point convergence in a control or numerical-analysis venue.
- Jaimungal-group numerical-game papers (Campbell et al. 2021) that lack a convergence theorem have stayed as preprints; Casgrain–Jaimungal (2020), which pairs an existence and characterisation result with an LSMC algorithm, was published in Mathematical Finance. This suggests finance journals accept a numerical algorithm when it is attached to a rigorous characterisation of the equilibrium.
- Balkin–Ceniceros–Hu (RNN per player plus fictitious play) is the closest prior architecture and is still a preprint after three years; the proposed method should state clearly what differs (non-nested observation filtrations, regression on first-order-condition targets, damped Picard rather than fictitious play).

### Gaps
- Reception beyond venue could not be measured: no verified citation counts, no reviews or editorials found.
- Journal status of Balkin–Ceniceros–Hu (2023), Campbell et al. (2021), Wu–Jaimungal (2024), Aqsha et al. (2024) and the two Guan–Shames–Summers papers (2026) is unknown or "working paper".
- SINUM and AAP volume and page numbers for Carmona–Laurière were not checked on the publisher pages.

## Key question 3: Which venues fit a method paper with finance examples and moderate theory?

### Takeaway
SIFIN is the best fit on scope (it explicitly invites "new methods and algorithms representing significant (as opposed to incremental) improvements") and on precedent (the broker–trader benchmark papers are there). Mathematical Finance requires numerical experiments to be "accompanied by a rigorous analysis in support of theoretical developments". Finance and Stochastics lists "numerical and stochastic solution techniques" and "uncertainty and information" in scope and published the broker–trader Nash paper. JMLR excludes "applications of machine learning to other domains"; ICML has accepted LQ-example deep-game papers; Mathematics of Operations Research has published a deep-learning Stackelberg MFG paper.

- Fit ranking for the described paper (scalar and low-dimensional LQ benchmarks, one new game without closed form, 3–8% relative L2 error, economic finding, moderate theory): SIFIN first (scope match, benchmark papers there, Short Communication option for a compact version); Finance and Stochastics second (published the broker–trader Nash paper; scope lists information and numerical techniques, but the journal's published game papers are closed-form); Mathematical Finance third, conditional on adding a rigorous result (for example, convergence of the damped Picard iteration or an error bound for the regression step).
- Journal of Computational Finance is a poor fit (pricing and risk focus). JMLR is a poor fit (finance application). ICML or NeurIPS would accept LQ examples, but the paper would need a learning-theoretic framing and larger-scale experiments than three scalar benchmarks.
- MOR or SICON fit if the contribution is reframed as a general algorithm for games with non-nested information structures and the control-literature gap (Guan–Shames–Summers, Li et al.) is the motivation; those audiences will expect a comparison to belief-truncation or common-information baselines.

### Gaps
- SIAM, Wiley and INFORMS scope pages could not be opened directly; quotations come from LetPub and search-engine summaries.
- No acceptance-rate or review-time data were found for any of the journals.
- Whether SIFIN has published a paper whose only examples are scalar LQ games with a deep-learning solver could not be established.

## Key question 4: What do reviewers in these venues typically require (theory, dimension, benchmarks)?

### Takeaway
No referee guidelines beyond the scope statements were found. The scope statements require "significant (as opposed to incremental)" algorithmic improvement (SIFIN) and numerical experiments "accompanied by a rigorous analysis" (Mathematical Finance). The structure of accepted deep-game papers shows the de facto standard: a convergence or ε-Nash result, validation on an LQ benchmark with an explicit solution, at least one example without closed form, and a scalability or dimension test.

### Inferences
- A SIFIN or Finance and Stochastics submission would likely be asked for: (i) a statement of what the damped Picard iteration converges to and under what conditions (a contraction or monotonicity argument on the FOC map, even for short horizons as in Wu–Jaimungal); (ii) an error decomposition separating time discretisation, regression (network) error and fixed-point error; (iii) a test beyond scalar games, for example the multi-asset Wu–Jaimungal setting, to show the method is not confined to the cases with closed forms; (iv) comparison with at least one alternative (deep fictitious play with a nested filtration, or a truncated-belief LQG solver). The 3–8% relative L2 errors are larger than those typically reported for deep BSDE-type solvers on LQ benchmarks (Hu–Laurière report DFP accuracy as "reasonable" on a 24-player LQ game without a figure in the summary); reviewers may ask whether error decreases with network size, sample size and iteration count.
- The economic finding (about 16% less trading under private signals) is the kind of conclusion finance referees expect from the numerical section (compare Casgrain–Jaimungal and Aqsha et al.).
- For an ML venue the expectations differ: scalability in players or dimension and ablations, rather than FBSDE theory.

### Gaps
- No explicit referee guidelines, editorials or reviewer checklists for numerical-method papers were found for SIFIN, Mathematical Finance, Quantitative Finance or Finance and Stochastics.
- Typical reported accuracy levels for deep game solvers on LQ benchmarks were not collected systematically; the comparison of 3–8% with the literature is an inference from the Hu–Laurière summary, not from tabulated numbers.
- No data on how referees treat low-dimensional (scalar) examples in SIFIN; the published SIFIN broker papers are closed-form, so they do not set a numerical standard.
